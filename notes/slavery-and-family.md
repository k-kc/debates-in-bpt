# Slave Narratives & Critical Context

## Gates, "Introduction," The Classic Slave Narratives

**Key Arguments:**
- Slave narratives ride Enlightenment discourse on "rights and reason" to make the decisive argument against human slavery
- Collective articulation of "The Talented Tenth"

**Critical Questions:**
- What's distinctive about "the slave narrative" as a form?
- What's the political legacy of the slave narrative?

**Key Phrases:**
- "testify against their captors"
- "peculiar kind of freedom--or burden--for the black person who was enslaved"

## Douglass, "Chapter I" Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass

### On Epistemological Deprivation

> "By far the larger part of the slaves know as little of their ages as horses know of theirs..."

**Note:** Pay attention to the horse comparison. What's Douglass doing by comparing enslaved people to horses?

### On Violated Kinship

> "My father was a white man. He was admitted to be such by all I ever heard speak of my parentage. The opinion was also whispered that my master was my father; but of the correctness of this opinion, I know nothing; the means of knowing was withheld from me."

**Note:** Pay attention to the cruel paternity—Douglass names the "double relation of master and father."

### On Maternal Absence

> "I never saw my mother, to know her as such, more than four or five times in my life; and each of these times was very short in duration, and at night. She was hired by a Mr. Stewart, who lived about twelve miles from my home. She made her journeys to see me in the night, travelling the whole distance on foot, after the performance of her day's work...I do not recollect of ever seeing my mother by the light of day."

**Note:** Pay attention to how Douglass describes his mother like an apparition. What effect does this nocturnal, spectral presence create?

### On Witnessing Violence

> "He would whip her to make her scream, and whip her to make her hush; and not until overcome by fatigue, would he cease to swing the blood-clotted cowskin. I remember the first time I ever witnessed this horrible exhibition. I was quite a child, but I well remember it. I never shall forget it whilst I remember any thing. It was the first of a long series of such outrages, of which I was doomed to be a witness and a participant. It struck me with awful force. It was the blood-stained gate, the entrance to the hell of slavery, through which I was about to pass."

**Note:** Pay attention to this as a baptism—a horrific threshold into the reality and meaning of slavery.

## Jacobs, "I. Childhood" Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl

### On Delayed Knowledge

> "I was born a slave; but I never know it till six years of happy childhood passed away."

**Note:** What does it mean that slavery was invisible to her initially?

### On Being Valued as Property

> "But as she grew older she evinced so much intelligence, and was so faithful, that her master and mistress could not help seeing it was for their interest to take care of such a valuable piece of property."

**Note:** Notice the strange pride here—being valued as property.

### On Intertwined Relations

> "My mother's mistress was the daughter of my grandmother's mistress. She was the foster sister of my mother; they were both nourished at my grandmother's breast. In fact, my mother had been weaned at three months old, that the babe of the mistress might obtain sufficient food. They played together as children; and, when they became women, my mother was a most faithful servant to her whiter foster sister."

**Note:** Notice the intertwined family trees and double relations of mistress and sister.

### On False Security

> "I was told that my home was now to be with her mistress; and I found it a happy one. No toilsome or disagreeable duties were imposed upon me. My mistress was so kind to me that I was always glad to do her bidding, and proud to labor for her as much as my young years would permit. I would sit by her side for hours, sewing diligently, with a heart as free from care as that of any free-born white child. When she thought I was tired, she would send me out to run and jump; and away I bounded, to gather berries or flowers to decorate her room. Those were happy days—too happy to last."

**Note:** Notice the ominous gothic ending—the darkness gathering. These days are too happy precisely because they cannot last.

### On Broken Promises

> "the will of my mistress was read, and we learned that she had bequeathed me to her sister's daughter, a child of five years old. So vanished our hopes. My mistress had taught me the precepts of God's Word: 'Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself.' 'Whatsoever ye would that men should do unto you, do ye even so unto them.' But I was her slave, and I suppose she did not recognize me as her neighbor."

**Note:** Notice the bitterness at a promise broken and a chance to recognize common humanity missed.

## Spillers, "Mama's Baby, Papa's Maybe" (1987)

### On Property and Kinlessness

Spillers argues that enslaved people occupy a unique position: not merely property, but property under a patriarchal order that denies kinship itself. The enslaved child becomes "the man/woman on the boundary, whose human and familial status, by the very nature of the case, had yet to be defined."

**Key concept:** "Enforced state of breach"—kinship loses meaning because it can be invaded at any moment by property relations.

### On Black Family as Achievement

Spillers rejects the pathologizing of Black family structures under slavery. Instead, she argues:

- The powerful ties of sympathy binding blood-relations was maintained despite slavery's prohibitions
- This was "one of the supreme social achievements of African-Americans under conditions of enslavement"
- Africans in the New World exercised "a degree of courage and will to survive that startles the imagination"

**Key move:** The Black family is not a pathology but a political and ethical achievement—an attempt to found "a people" on something other than biology.

### On Biological vs. Social Kinship

Spillers distinguishes between Western kinship (vertical transfer of bloodline, patronymic, entitlements, property) and the forced disruption of that system under slavery.

**Key argument:** Under slavery, "genetic reproduction becomes, then, not an elaboration of the life-principle in its cultural overlap, but an extension of the boundaries of proliferating properties."

### On Female Vulnerability

Spillers argues that enslavement creates a unique vulnerability for Black women:

- The "open exchange of female bodies" under slavery provides an "Ur-text" to understanding gendered female representation
- The enslaved female becomes "ungendered"—vulnerable to invasion/raiding by male or female, master or mistress
- The mistress (particularly in Jacobs' narrative) becomes a metaphor for madness arising from unchecked power

### On Constructing Kinship

Spillers asks a crucial question: Is kinship feeling "inevitable," or does it describe a relationship that appears natural but must be "cultivated" under actual material conditions?

**Key insight:** Enslaved people developed "certain ethical and sentimental features that tied her and him, across the landscape to others, often sold from hand to hand, of the same and different blood in a common fabric of memory and inspiration."

### On Violated Paternity

Spillers examines how paternity functions under slavery as a "supreme cultural courtesy" that becomes a "monstrous accumulation of power."

**Key move:** The denied genetic link becomes the chief strategy of undenied ownership. Fatherhood becomes an empty totem—the blank space where the proper name should fit is answered by the fact of material possession.

**Crucial passage:** "These 'threads cable-strong' of an incestuous, interracial genealogy uncover slavery in the United States as one of the richest displays of the psychoanalytic dimensions of culture before the science of European psychoanalysis takes hold."

## Cross-Text Themes

### Epistemology & Knowledge
- Douglass: ignorance of age, paternity, mother imposed as a condition of slavery
- Jacobs: delayed knowledge of her own enslavement
- Spillers: the denial of kinship relations as a form of epistemic violence

### Kinship Violated & Reconstructed
- Douglass: double relation of master and father; maternal absence as spectral presence
- Jacobs: intertwined family trees; foster-sister relations; bequest to a child rather than to her
- Spillers: kinship as an achievement; constructed kinship across sold bodies and different blood

### Gender & Sexuality Under Slavery
- Douglass: the master's double relation to enslaved women
- Jacobs: the jealous mistress; female vulnerability to multiple forms of violation
- Spillers: the ungendered female; pansexual potential and vulnerability; the mistress as prosthetic to male power

### Testimony & Witnessing
- Douglass: witnessing violence as a baptismal entry into slavery's meaning
- Jacobs: recognizing the broken promise to recognize common humanity
- Spillers: slave narratives as psychoanalytic documents that uncover the dimensions of enslavement
